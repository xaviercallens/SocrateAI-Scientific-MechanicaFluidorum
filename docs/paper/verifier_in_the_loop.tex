\begin{abstract}
Large language models produce mathematics and physics that read well and are sometimes wrong,
and the discipline that has grown up around them is verification: proof assistants for proofs,
and now public registries that check whether a repository proves what it claims. That discipline
is necessary and it stops at the proof. A scientific laboratory also chooses questions, runs
experiments, reads numbers, writes papers and publishes them, and none of those steps is
certified by a kernel. We describe a working method that extends verifier-in-the-loop practice
to the whole of that loop, and we report what it did over one month in two small laboratories
working on the three-dimensional Navier--Stokes equations: one running the method in full on a
machine-checked programme for the frequency-truncated system, the other auditing an
AI-generated, Lean-certified blow-up proof and arriving independently at two of the same
instruments. The method has four instruments --- a tiered epistemic gate with formal proof at
the top and exact rational arithmetic below it, a control discipline for every computational
measurement, pre-registration of every prediction before the run that tests it, and a normative
claim ledger outside of which nothing may be cited --- and an agent framework in which model
tiers, permissions and reusable skills are assigned by the cost of a wrong statement rather than
by the difficulty of the task. The evidence is a record of failures: over the month, all but three of
the recorded errors occurred with both mechanical gates (the kernel and the exact-arithmetic
harness) green, and those were caught by a
control, a pre-registered prediction, an external audit, or an adversarial review of a
manuscript against its own formal sources; the three exceptions are the gate catching exactly
what it is built to catch, and once catching too much. We list the incidents, what caught each
one, and the rule each produced. The method does not make an AI laboratory correct. It makes
its errors legible, dated, and cheap, which is what a laboratory needs. Nothing here is a claim
about fluid mechanics or about the regularity of the Navier--Stokes equations; the two
laboratories are the setting, not the result.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In the last two years the question ``can a machine do mathematics?'' has been replaced by a
harder one: ``how do we know when it has?'' Terence Tao's account of machine-assisted proof
\cite{tao2025} sets the frame: machine assistance is not new, but its scale and nature are
changing, and the response of the community has been to make the machine's output checkable ---
by a proof assistant whose kernel is small enough to trust, and now by a public registry, Palomar
\cite{palomar2026}, that records a claim, compiles its Lean proof against a fixed library with
only permitted axioms, and asks whether the formal statement is a fair rendering of the informal
one. The Equational Theories Project \cite{etp2025} showed what that trust model makes possible:
thousands of implications settled by contributors who did not need to trust each other, because
every one was kernel-checked. In September 2026 an AI system produced Lean-certified proofs of
finite-time blow-up for the forced Navier--Stokes equations and for the unforced Euler equations
\cite{openai2026}, and the discussion that followed turned, correctly, on what the formal
statements say and whether they mean what the informal ones claim.

This paper is about the part of science that the kernel does not see. A laboratory does not
only prove; it decides what to measure, builds an instrument, runs it, reads the number, decides
what the number means, writes it up and publishes it. Every step is a place where an AI system
--- or a human --- can be fluent and wrong, and only the first step has a mechanical gate. Tao's
own advice for the rest is a working rule rather than a tool: only rely on AI where you are
able to red-team its output \cite{taoviews2026}. We take that rule as a design requirement and ask what
a laboratory has to build so that the red-teaming happens by construction, before a result is
load-bearing, and so that when it fails --- as it will --- the failure is recorded rather than
absorbed.

Our answer is a method with four instruments and an agent framework, and our evidence is a
month of running it. The instruments are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item a \textbf{tiered epistemic gate}: every claim carries a tier fixed by the strongest check
  it has passed --- a kernel-checked proof with a clean axiom footprint, an identity verified in
  exact rational arithmetic with a control demonstrated to fail, or a conjecture --- and a
  floating-point number can never rise above the lowest tier (Section~\ref{sec:tiers});
\item a \textbf{control discipline} for computational measurement that treats the instrument,
  the control, and the code that decides ``did this succeed'' as claim-bearing artefacts
  needing their own evidence (Section~\ref{sec:controls});
\item \textbf{pre-registration} of every prediction, with its bracket and its factors, committed
  to version control before the run that tests it, and a rule that a falsified registration is
  reported as loudly as a confirmed one (Section~\ref{sec:prereg});
\item a normative \textbf{claim ledger} and a \textbf{lessons file}: a claim absent from the
  ledger has no tier and may not be cited; a rule absent from the lessons file has no reason and
  will be shortcut (Section~\ref{sec:ledger}).
\end{enumerate}

The agent framework (Section~\ref{sec:agents}) assigns model tiers, permissions and reusable
procedures --- ``skills'' --- to steps according to a single principle: \emph{the cost of a
wrong statement sets the tier}. Statement selection and audit sit at the top with the human;
mechanical, gate-verifiable work sits at the bottom with the cheapest model; and the harness
that runs the agents is itself an instrument that must be audited by something other than the
agent it constrains.

The evidence (Section~\ref{sec:evidence}) is deliberately a record of what went wrong. Across
the month, in both laboratories, the formal gates were green through nearly every substantive
error; the exceptions are the cases the gate exists for. The rest were caught by controls, by pre-registered predictions, by an external audit of what
a formal statement actually referred to, and by an adversarial review of a manuscript against
the Lean statements it cited. We list them with what caught each and what rule it produced. We
then state the method as a protocol (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}), in the spirit of Tao's
proposals but extended from proofs to experiments and to the boundary between a mathematical
model and the physical system it is a model of, and we close with what the month did not
establish (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\paragraph{Scope.} Nothing here is a claim about fluid mechanics. The mathematical results of
the first laboratory are published separately with their own audit \cite{socrateai2026triad};
the physical reading produced by the second is published with its own changelog of withdrawn
claims \cite{callens2026reading}. This paper cites both only as evidence about method. The
open problem both laboratories work near --- global regularity of the three-dimensional
Navier--Stokes equations --- remains open, and nothing in either laboratory bears on it.

\section{Related practice}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Formal proof as the trust layer.} Flyspeck \cite{hales2017} established that a
proof too long for referees can be made trustworthy by a kernel; Buzzard's survey
\cite{buzzard2024} describes how proof assistants and language models began to meet; Tao
\cite{tao2025} argues that the change is in scale and in the trust model, and the Equational
Theories Project \cite{etp2025} is the existence proof: an implication graph on $4{,}694$
laws settled by mixed human and automated proofs, all validated in Lean. Palomar
\cite{palomar2026} makes the trust layer a public service with two automated checks --- a
mechanical one, that the proof typechecks and proves the recorded statement against a fixed
library with permitted axioms only; and a non-deterministic one, in which a language model
judges whether the informal description in the submission's disclosure file matches the
formal claim and the repository meets minimal standards --- and its announcement states
plainly that these checks fall well short of peer review. Our tier system (Section~\ref{sec:tiers}) implements the first check
locally and departs from the second: we reserve statement adequacy to human audit, for reasons
the evidence section makes concrete.

\paragraph{Search with a verifier in the loop.} AlphaProof \cite{alphaproof2025} learns to
prove in a formal environment where every step is checked; FunSearch \cite{funsearch2024}
pairs a language model with a systematic evaluator to search program space and found new
constructions in extremal combinatorics. Both rely on the evaluator being incorruptible and
cheap. Karpathy's \emph{autoresearch} \cite{karpathy2026} applies the same shape to
engineering: an agent proposes one change at a time, an immutable evaluator scores it under a
fixed budget, and a mechanical rule keeps or discards. Section~\ref{sec:agents} adapts that
loop to a laboratory, with the changes the control discipline forces on it.

\paragraph{Pre-registration.} Nosek and colleagues \cite{nosek2018} argue that registering a
prediction before the data exist is what distinguishes a test from a fit. Computational science
has been slow to adopt it because a simulation can be re-run; we found that this is exactly why
it is needed there (Section~\ref{sec:prereg}).

\paragraph{Rigorous numerics.} Where a computation is meant to be part of a proof, the
standard is interval arithmetic with a self-consistent a-priori bound, as in
Zgliczy\'nski--Mischaikow \cite{zgliczynski2001}. Our exact-rational tier is the discrete
analogue for identities, and our experience with iterating nonlinear maps in exact arithmetic
(Section~\ref{sec:physics}) is a caution about where the analogue stops.

\section{The tiered gate}
\label{sec:tiers}

Every claim in the laboratory carries one of four tiers, fixed by the strongest mechanical
check it has passed, and enforced by one script that either exits zero or does not. The
formal tier is Lean~4 \cite{lean4} against a pinned revision of Mathlib \cite{mathlib}.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{1.6cm}p{5.6cm}p{7.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Tier} & \textbf{Meaning} & \textbf{Gate} \\
\midrule
\tier{A} & Established: a Lean~4 theorem, kernel-compiled against a pinned Mathlib revision. &
Zero \texttt{sorry}; the compiled theorem's axiom footprint, printed by the kernel, names no
axiom outside $\{\texttt{propext},\texttt{Classical.choice},\texttt{Quot.sound}\}$. The gate is
the footprint on the compiled artefact, not the absence of the word \texttt{sorry} in the
source. \\
\tier{B} & Checkable: an identity or inequality verified in exact rational arithmetic on
concrete instances; a certified witness. & A harness using integers and rationals only ---
floating point is banned from the test directory --- that exits zero, and that ships a negative
control demonstrated to fail and, for any harness returning a verdict rather than asserting an
identity, a positive control demonstrated to fire. \\
\tier{L} & Literature: a published theorem, cited to its statement with the hypotheses that
scope it. & A citation helper that refuses a numerical threshold lacking either its source or
its hypotheses. A \tier{L} row may motivate, scope or refute; it never discharges a formal
obligation. \\
\tier{C} & Conjecture: analogy, physical narrative, unverified reduction, and every
floating-point computation. & None, but the tag is mandatory, and \tier{C} code lives in a
quarantined directory that nothing above it imports. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Three features of this table carry most of its value, and each was learned by getting it wrong.

\paragraph{The gate is a membership test on the compiled artefact.} A failed Lean proof still
defines its theorem name in the environment; only the printed footprint reveals the hidden
\texttt{sorryAx}. An external submission to the first laboratory claimed four theorems ``with no
\texttt{sorry} placeholders''; compiled as received, one carried a custom axiom and one did not
compile at all, and its environment carried \texttt{sorryAx} with no literal \texttt{sorry} in
the file. The rule that followed --- archive every submission verbatim, compile it exactly as
received, save the kernel log as the evidentiary record --- is the same rule Palomar
implements as its first check. The gate itself was also wrong once: it string-matched the exact
three-axiom list, so a lemma proved by pure computation, whose footprint was the strictly
cleaner $[\texttt{propext}]$, was rejected. That false positive is not cosmetic. The cheapest
way to satisfy an equality gate is to \emph{add} an unnecessary axiom, so the gate would have
trained proofs to get worse in the one dimension it measures. It is now a membership test, and
the change --- a loosening of a verification gate, exactly where a plausible edit can silently
stop catching things --- was accepted only after a six-case table including cases that must
fail.

\paragraph{The verifier is pinned.} The first cold build of the Lean project revealed that its
dependency file required Mathlib from git with no revision. A fresh clone would have resolved to
master and verified every ledger claim against a different library than the one they were
checked with. For a programme whose entire epistemic claim is machine-checkability, an unpinned
prover is a defect in the foundation. The library is pinned by revision and the lockfile is
tracked.

\paragraph{Tier A attaches to the formal statement, never to its gloss.} This is what the
laboratory's specification calls the honesty clause, and it is where the method departs from a
registry that lets a language model judge
statement adequacy. The largest failure in the first laboratory's history was not a proof error.
For weeks its specification, its report and its audit packet asserted that its formalisation
``restates the open core of the Millennium problem''. An external expert audit found that it did
not: a one-dimensional index cutoff, a weight $4^n$ and an unconstrained interaction term
describe a dyadic shell hierarchy, not the three-dimensional equations. Two machine gates, five
governing documents and an adversarial self-review had all passed, because none of them can
check whether a formal statement refers to the object it names. The rule is that adequacy of a
statement is certified by human audit sought early and from outside, and that a file marked
\textsc{draft} pending that audit may not have its gloss repeated in prose as if the audit had
passed. A registry's language-model check is a useful filter; it is not this.

\section{The control discipline}
\label{sec:controls}

A computational measurement has three parts that can be wrong independently of the science: the
instrument, the control that is supposed to prove the instrument works, and the code that decides
whether the run succeeded. The method treats all three as claim-bearing artefacts that need
their own evidence. The rules below were each written after an incident, and the incidents are
in Section~\ref{sec:evidence}.

\paragraph{A checker that cannot fail is not a checker --- and that is half the requirement.}
Every harness ships a negative control: an input on which it must fail, and the failure is
demonstrated, not assumed. The other half was learned when a structure detector shipped with
an inverted sign and passed its negative control cleanly --- generic inputs read $O(1)$ under
both the correct and the inverted formula. Only a \emph{positive} control, an input carrying the
structure by construction and required to read zero, exposed it. Inverted, it would have
reported ``no structure'' everywhere, including where the structure existed: a false negative
indistinguishable from a clean null. Identity verifiers satisfy the positive direction by
construction (the main sweep asserts the identity holds); classifiers and detectors do not, and
for them an explicit positive control is mandatory.

\paragraph{Four ways a passing control is still worthless.} A control tests the code against
the premise the author believes. It cannot test the belief. The laboratory's own catalogue:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Wrong premise.} A regime classifier's positive-control anchor was a published
  threshold quoted from an abstract; the theorem statement, retrieved later, placed the
  threshold elsewhere and carried a positivity hypothesis the abstract did not mention. Both
  controls fired for a day on a wrong verdict. Rule: a numerical threshold is cited to the
  theorem statement with its hypotheses, never to the abstract, and a regression control pins the
  value that was wrong so the old belief cannot return.
\item \emph{Violated hypothesis.} A theorem-backed control failed because the run's initial
  data violated the theorem's hypothesis (infinite enstrophy in the limit), not because the code
  was wrong. Rule: encode the hypothesis as a runtime refusal, not a comment.
\item \emph{Ambiguous instrument state.} ``Stopped early'' meant both ``the solution is blowing
  up'' and ``the step budget ran out'', and a summary read the second as the first in a regime
  where blow-up is a theorem to be impossible. Rule: distinct stop reasons get distinct names,
  and an aggregate containing a resource-limit stop refuses to be interpreted.
\item \emph{Undemonstrated perturbation.} A negative control's perturbation looked destructive
  and preserved the symmetry under test. Rule: a negative control is a claim carrying the same
  burden of proof as any other; and a failed negative control is often informative --- this one
  became a better positive control.
\end{itemize}
Two corollaries do most of the work in practice. Prefer a control whose expected answer is a
\emph{published theorem}: it cannot be argued with, and in the August cycle every real catch
by a control came from one. And when a control fails, the first hypothesis is that the
control or the run is wrong --- not the code, and never the world.

\paragraph{An instrument must report its own reliability.} A fit, an inversion or a solve
must report its conditioning alongside its value and refuse to report a value when the
computation is ill-posed. The incident: a detector produced a clean, monotone, step-size-convergent
time series confirming the hypothesis the laboratory existed to find; the fit behind it was
singular at early times and the detector was dividing noise by noise. Convergence under
refinement does not help --- a singular fit converges to the same garbage at every step size.
The rule that survives is: be most suspicious of the result you wanted.

\paragraph{The code that decides ``did this succeed'' is an instrument too.} A month's most
expensive computation --- an exact evaluation on rented hardware --- converged correctly and was
then declared failed twice by its own verification code: once because a plain text search
treated a log containing a stray null byte (an artefact the log's own unflushed design can
produce) as binary and silently matched nothing; once because a resume path read a field that a
\emph{completed} checkpoint never writes. Both times the policy ``discard as corrupt, restart
from scratch'' fired on a byte-for-byte valid checkpoint, and the second time it began a
sixteen-and-a-half-hour recompute on a paid instance. Rules: success-detection code gets a negative
control of its own, exercised on every state the process can be resumed into, including
``already finished''; ``discard'' needs proof that the \emph{data} are corrupt, obtained by
reading the artefact with a different tool than the one that just failed on it; and a result
that is independently backed up is \emph{recovered}, not re-earned. The corrected binary, run
locally against the mirrored checkpoint, reproduced the identical result in under a second.

\section{Pre-registration, and reading numbers}
\label{sec:prereg}

The laboratory adopted pre-registration for computational measurements after reading a
three-point series wrongly. The series was reported as ``decelerating, consistent with
saturation'' from its successive ratios ($\times5.3$, $\times2.2$). Its differences were
growing ($+0.0099$, $+0.0143$). Fitting the generic approach-to-a-ceiling form returned an
inadmissible exponent: no saturating curve passes through the points at all. The reassuring
reading came entirely from choosing one of two summaries of the same three numbers.

The registration procedure that followed has five steps and one non-negotiable property. State
the rival hypotheses as parameterised functional forms, not as an observable (any number
answers ``does it grow?''). Fit every form to the points in hand and report inadmissible
parameters. Report the trend in the units the claim is about --- ratios and differences, side by
side --- and look for a bookkeeping mechanism that would produce the trend with none of the
physics (a fraction climbing to a hard ceiling did most of the ``deceleration'' here). Predict
the next point with a bracket, by two routes where possible, and \emph{pre-register the factors
of a product rather than the composite}: an observable that is a product of separately
measurable factors loses exactly the information that makes the prediction worth making when
its composite is extrapolated. Then check that the prediction can discriminate: evaluate every
rival at the point about to be run, and if the predictions overlap, say so before spending the
machine. The non-negotiable property is the commit timestamp: the registration is written into
the governing memo and committed before the run launches. A prediction written after the result
and presented as registered destroys the entire value of the practice.

What this delivered in the month was not correct predictions. Of three registered brackets on
one series, two were falsified --- the first below the bracket, the second above --- and the
third held. Each falsification localised to one named factor of the product (once the
injection, once the survival fraction), so the post-mortem produced a better model instead of
an argument. The lesson file records the sequel honestly: on this family, no three-point
extrapolation survived the next doubling, three times running, and the admissibility test that
seemed decisive on three points reversed sign on four and again on five. An exact fit to as many
points as parameters carries almost no information about the next point; report it with its
parameter count attached, and never let it settle a question one more point can reverse. A
falsified pre-registration is a success of the method and must be reported as loudly as a
confirmed one --- louder, since the confirmed ones are the ones nobody checks.

\section{The ledger, the lessons file, and context as an artefact}
\label{sec:ledger}

Two documents make the method hold across many sessions of many agents, none of which
remembers the last.

The \textbf{ledger} is normative: a claim not listed in it has no tier and may not be cited. It
is checked mechanically on every verification run --- every repository path it cites must
exist, every Lean name in a top-tier row must be declared, and no versioned duplicate files may
have appeared --- and the checker ships its own controls (a dangling path, a dangling name and a banned filename must
each be rejected, and a clean case must be accepted). It found a real defect on its first
run.

The \textbf{lessons file} records, for every process rule, the incident that produced it, with
evidence, what it cost or nearly cost, and where the rule now lives. It exists because a rule
whose reason is lost is a rule that will be shortcut the next time it is inconvenient. Its
entries are the primary source for Section~\ref{sec:evidence}; the file has thirty-one of them
after a month, and two syntheses --- the four ways a passing control is worthless, and the three
blind spots of a two-gate system (cost, interpretation, and the harness itself) --- that were
written when the entries had accumulated into a pattern.

Context, in this method, is not held in any agent's memory. It is externalised into five
documents with a strict precedence --- the specification, the operational plan, the ledger, the
lessons, and the build manual --- and every session loads them before acting. The report of the
first laboratory places this instruction first, because acting without it had already caused
documented failures.

\section{Mathematics and physics: the model, the simulation, and the boundary}
\label{sec:physics}

A laboratory that does both proof and experiment needs a stated relation between them, and the
month produced three rules.

\paragraph{Floating point is Tier C, always.} Simulation is indispensable for steering and
inadmissible as evidence. The scout code that measures transients in the truncated system was
calibrated against eight steps of an exact rational integrator to $2.4\times10^{-16}$ before any
of its numbers were read, and every one of its results carries the lowest tier no matter how
clean it looks. The exact integrator itself could not go further: iterating a nonlinear map in
exact arithmetic is a representation-size problem, not a stability problem --- denominators
reached tens of thousands of bits within a handful of steps while the magnitudes stayed modest
--- and a second numerical design that fixed the stiffness diagnosed from the first failed anyway,
because it had fixed the wrong axis. The rule: exact arithmetic for one-shot algebraic
certification, floating point for trajectories, and never confuse a divergence in representation
size with a divergence in the quantity.

\paragraph{Physical narrative is quarantined.} The motivating physics of the first laboratory
lives in a directory that the formal sources never import and that no tier promotion may cite.
The sense of analogy that motivates a programme is welcome there; it earns its way into the core
only through the exact and formal tiers. This is a stronger separation than most programmes keep,
and it was tested: at least four proposed physical mechanisms were killed on measured
evidence within the month, and none of the kills required touching a theorem.

\paragraph{A theorem about a model is labelled, not rejected.} The second laboratory's subject
is a Lean-certified proof that a forced incompressible fluid blows up in finite time. Its
current position, after a sequence of withdrawn stronger claims, is that the constructed flow
leaves the validity range of the incompressible continuum model --- at the single scale where
Mach, Knudsen and Eckert numbers all become order one --- a few picoseconds before the
mathematical singularity for water, nanoseconds for air. The formal counterpart is proved in Lean on the original proof's own definitions:
every object with the construction's properties exceeds every velocity-gradient bound
arbitrarily close to the singular time. The methodological point is the framing the laboratory
arrived at after discarding its first one: a physical-validity layer should \emph{label} a
result as a theorem about a model rather than a statement about a fluid; it should not reject a
correct proof. Its early manifesto proposed a ``\texttt{physlib}'' world model under which
``the AI will automatically discard candidate proofs'' that are physically implausible, and
its current status note withdraws that phrase. The proof is correct. The question of which model it is about is a scientific question,
answered by a predicate --- here a bound on local vorticity equivalent to Mach and Knudsen
bounds together --- that is stated, formalised where it can be, and reported with the theorem.

The same laboratory states the discipline in one sentence its programme document uses as a
design requirement: ``a lock without a test is a slogan.'' Every proposed lock --- its term for
a physical mechanism that would arrest the collapse --- is written with what it is, whether it
is derived or posited, what it predicts, and what could be formalised. And it applied that standard to its own proposed remedy, a cutoff regularisation,
and found that the remedy could not be evaluated at all with generic data --- ``it is easier to
identify where a model fails than to establish that a proposed repair does anything,'' its paper
records, ``and the standard of evidence we applied to [the original] construction is the one we
then had to apply to our own suggestion.''

\section{The agent framework}
\label{sec:agents}

\subsection{Roles by the cost of a wrong statement}

The framework assigns work to model tiers and to the human by one principle: the cost of a
wrong statement sets the tier. In the first laboratory's adjudicated workflow:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item the \textbf{human owner} supplies definitions and verdicts --- what is to be proved,
  whether a formal statement is adequate, whether a result is accepted, where doubt and compute
  are spent --- and never the content of a proof or a script;
\item the \textbf{top model tier} writes derivations, statements and audits: the hand
  derivation and the fully specified proof skeleton that precede any dispatch, the adversarial
  review of a claim or a manuscript, the escalation when a task needs a definition that does
  not exist;
\item the \textbf{middle tier} implements from fully specified memos --- a proof against
  confirmed-available lemmas, a harness against a stated identity --- and is not asked to
  decide what to prove;
\item the \textbf{cheapest tier} does gate-verifiable mechanics, where a wrong output is caught
  by the next gate at no cost.
\end{itemize}
The order is never reversed to save a step. Every proof that closed on the first or second
attempt in the month was preceded by a hand derivation, a numeric instance, and a check that the
named library lemmas were actually built; tasks that asked an agent to \emph{choose} the
statement either escalated correctly or needed a human decision partway through.

\subsection{The screen: autoresearch with controls}

When several plausible variants of a design exist and no principled reason prefers one, the
framework runs a fixed-budget screen after Karpathy's pattern \cite{karpathy2026}, with three
changes the control discipline forces. First, the hypotheses are proposed by several independent
agents of a cheaper tier, each given the same facts and a different lens (optimisation, cost,
controls), \emph{without access to the code}; a stronger-tier judge synthesises the final list,
the control rows, and a mechanical decision rule, and all of it is committed before the first
row runs. Second --- the step that caught the real bugs --- the authoring session checks every
proposal against the code path it presumes to describe before the design becomes binding. In the
month's one screen this found two proposals that assumed a mechanism the code provably does not
have, and a genuine convergence bug in the optimiser being screened, visible only because a
proposal's assumption prompted a direct read of the loop it reasoned about. A panel review is a
review of the description, not of the code. Third, the loop does not rewrite its own hypotheses:
when a registered rule misfired on real results, it was run as registered, both readings were
reported, and the alternative was added as an explicitly post-hoc row that could not win.
Changing the rule after seeing the table is the thing pre-registration exists to prevent; hiding
the misfire is worse.

\subsection{The harness is an instrument, audited from outside}

Three rules concern the machinery that runs the agents rather than the agents.

\emph{A permission boundary is enforced by the boundary.} A session denied the ability to edit
its own permission file observed that an allowed general-purpose file-rewriting command would
have let it rewrite that file regardless, and reported the denial instead of using the opening.
The rule is not ``agents should be restrained''; it is that an allow-list containing a general
rewriter does not deny anything, and must be audited for rules that subsume the denials ---
before granting unattended overnight compute, which is when nobody is watching. The same
session, during a later overnight run, had a cloud-provisioning tool remain denied for the
run's whole duration despite the owner adding it to the allow-list, because the settings
watcher does not reload without a restart; it reported the limit twice rather than working
around it.

\emph{Blast radius is scoped.} Any command whose effect is the whole working tree --- a broad
\texttt{git add}, a \texttt{stash}, a \texttt{reset} --- is forbidden while any concurrent
process may be writing. A broad add twice swept a mid-edit file into a commit; the second time
it was a proof, and the commit message, written before the concurrent state was known, said
``both gates pass'' --- true for the files checked and false for the one incidentally captured. Concurrent tools work in their own
worktree and merge back through review; a file modified in the main checkout by anything other
than its owning session is an escalation, not a merge.

\emph{Self-reports are not evidence.} A tool's own status field, a subagent's final report, a
submission's ``no sorry by construction'', the deposit script's own \texttt{PUBLISHED} line: each
is re-run or re-fetched on the exact artefact before it is believed. The month's cases include a
subagent whose final report would have said ``done, compiles clean'' --- truthfully --- and never
mentioned the repository-wide stash it had run in the middle.

\subsection{The second laboratory}

The second laboratory audits an AI-generated, Lean-certified proof \cite{openai2026}. Its
current position \cite{callens2026reading} is that the proof is correct and that the
constructed flow leaves the validity range of the incompressible model before the singularity.
At its archived version 5.6.0 it holds ten Lean files with $85$ declarations with verified
axiom footprints, one of them stated on the original proof's own definitions; a
reproducibility benchmark that re-runs every quoted number and compares it bit-for-bit with
the committed value ($54$ checks at the preceding release); and a $31$-page paper with a
concept DOI (its working source has since moved to 5.6.1). It is run by the same owner as the
first laboratory, with no shared code or method. It did not adopt the first laboratory's
method. It converged independently on two of its instruments --- the kernel gate with the same
axiom footprint, and a changelog in which corrections are recorded rather than rewritten ---
and it made the opposite choice on a third: it has no exact-arithmetic tier, every simulation
is floating point, and what stands in for exactness is bit-identical reproducibility on a
pinned software stack, a weaker property that the benchmark states explicitly. Its enforced
continuous-integration workflow is a stale legacy pipeline whose runs fail at startup; the
current Lean check is run on demand. That makes it a useful second data point rather than a
replication.

Its evidence for method is the changelog and the paper's appendix on previous versions, which
sorts what changed under six headings: claims withdrawn, material removed, citations
corrected, reframed not retracted, withdrawn in a later version, and forecasts not borne out.
Table~\ref{tab:sister} lists the entries that bear on method, with what caught each. Three
deserve comment. A ``Lighthill energy-budget check'' had been presented as an independent
confirmation of the Mach criterion; dimensional analysis showed the two were the same
criterion, so the corroboration was a single check counted twice. A global enstrophy constant
used as an admissibility axiom since early versions was withdrawn when someone asked where it
came from and found it ``had no derivation anywhere in the repository''; it had propagated
through papers, code, a table generator and Lean docstrings. And an ``interception theorem''
whose file claimed ``0 sorry'' in prose turned out, on re-compilation, not to build against the
current toolchain at all --- and its statement was circular besides: its admissibility
hypothesis contained the withdrawn constant, so at best it would have proved only that a
divergent quantity is not bounded. Two rules in one artefact: a self-reported kernel check is
not a kernel check, and a kernel, had it checked it, would not have noticed that the
antecedent contained the conclusion.

The changelog also records two things the first laboratory's method now borrows. On 15
September the project's Zenodo upload script still carried the title, description and file

a permanent identifier. The same day the project found its public dataset already serving the
retracted paper and the unit bug, live, under a real name. Both were caught by reading before

commit, metadata read against the paper, one stale sentence caught in that very check --- is
what our publish gate encodes. And the project's peer review, recorded verbatim with a
point-by-point response, contains an editorial observation worth passing on: the reviewer
asked that the manuscript's running comparisons with ``earlier drafts'' be moved into an
appendix --- that transparency be \emph{relocated}, not reduced --- and the authors did so while

extraction artefact of the reviewer's own copy, one a genuine defect, and two were rebuilt for

reading of that comparison overstated the molecular numbers and was itself corrected in the
changelog --- which is the discipline working, not failing: the registration is dated, and the
correction is dated after it.

\subsection{What the two laboratories show together}

Three things, and the third is the finding of this paper.

First, the tiered gate does what it is for and no more. Across both laboratories, no theorem
promoted to the top tier has been found wrong, and every attempt to promote a statement that was
not adequate was caught --- once by an external audit, once by the laboratory's own honesty
clause refusing to let a \textsc{draft} gloss stand as a claim. The gate bounds what may be
\emph{claimed}.

Second, the gate is silent on what should be \emph{asked}, what it should \emph{cost}, and
whether the \emph{instrument} is sound. All but three entries in Table~\ref{tab:incidents}
occurred with both gates green. The instruments that caught them --- controls, registered predictions, cost
models, external audit, adversarial review --- are cheap, mechanical, and none of them is
clever. Their common form is the conversion of an assertion into a counted or measured
quantity before it becomes load-bearing.

Two of the same errors occurred in both laboratories independently, which is the closest thing
this paper has to a replication. Each quoted a Lean theorem count under one convention and
later found it was another (``45 theorems'' that were $36$ by the convention adopted; a
report's $258$ that the gate certifies as $193$), and each fixed it the same way, by stating
the convention and taking the number from the gate's own transcript. And each carried a
transcription error from an internal record into a manuscript --- a ``seven-point'' series
that has six points, a hand-placed figure labelled as computed --- caught only by tracing the
number past the record to the file that produced it. Counts drift with conventions and
transcriptions drift with distance from the source; neither is a proof error, and both reached
a manuscript.

Third, the method's product is not correctness but \emph{legibility}. Both laboratories were
wrong repeatedly. The difference the method made is that each error is dated, localised to a
named factor or a named line, attributed to the mechanism that caught it, and attached to a
rule --- and that the wrong reading was never available to be published, because the ledger
had no row for it. A laboratory cannot promise to be right. It can promise that its errors will
be this shape.

\section{The proposal: a protocol for an AI-assisted laboratory}
\label{sec:protocol}

We state the method as rules, in the order a new laboratory would adopt them. Each is a
consequence of an incident above; none is a preference.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Tier every claim by the strongest check it has passed, and enforce the tier by
  a script that exits non-zero.} Formal proof with a clean footprint on the compiled artefact;
  exact arithmetic with demonstrated controls; literature cited to the theorem statement with
  its hypotheses; everything else, including all floating point, conjecture.
\item \textbf{Keep a normative ledger.} A claim not in it may not be cited. Check it
  mechanically for orphans on every run.
\item \textbf{Reserve statement adequacy to a human, sought early and from outside.} The kernel
  checks the proof; no gate checks that the statement refers to the object it names. A
  language-model check is a filter, not a certificate.
\item \textbf{Derive by hand before dispatching; verify downstream anyway.} The top tier writes
  the statement and the skeleton; the implementer executes; the certifier reproduces the memo's
  own numbers before running the sweep --- because the memo can be wrong.
\item \textbf{Every measurement ships both controls, and the controls carry their own burden
  of proof.} Negative and positive; premise cited to a theorem; hypotheses enforced at runtime;
  stop reasons named; perturbations demonstrated. Prefer a control whose expected answer is a
  published theorem.
\item \textbf{Instruments report their own reliability and refuse when ill-posed.} Be most
  suspicious of the result you wanted.
\item \textbf{Pre-register the prediction --- as factors, with a bracket, with a control where
  the effect must be absent, with a discrimination check --- and commit it before the run.}
  Report a falsification as loudly as a confirmation. Never present a post-diction as a
  registration.
\item \textbf{State the cost model before scaling; count, never estimate; report measured
  factors, not operation counts.} A correct answer is camouflage for a bad algorithm, and a
  rented machine conceals cost failures rather than exposing them.
\item \textbf{Treat verification code, checkpoint readers and success detectors as
  instruments.} Give them negative controls on every state they can encounter. Before
  discarding data, prove the data are corrupt with a different tool. Recover backed-up results
  rather than re-earning them.
\item \textbf{Quarantine physical narrative and floating point; label theorems about a model
  as such.} A physical-validity predicate states which model a theorem is about; it never
  rejects a correct proof.
\item \textbf{Assign model tiers, permissions and skills by the cost of a wrong statement.}
  Enforce permission boundaries by the boundary; audit allow-lists for rules that subsume
  denials; scope every tool's blast radius; treat self-reports as not evidence.
\item \textbf{Audit every manuscript against its formal sources and against retrieved
  literature before it leaves; gate every permanent identifier behind a checklist that the
  verification gates do not cover.} Author of record supplied, artefact matched to the tag,
  metadata read against the withdrawn list, draft before publish, live record verified without
  credentials.
\item \textbf{Record every rule with its incident.} A rule without its reason will be
  shortcut; a laboratory without a lessons file repeats its month.
\end{enumerate}

For a referee of an AI-assisted paper, the protocol reduces to four questions. Which tier does
each claim carry, and is the gate a script or a sentence? Where is the registration commit for
each prediction, and what happened to the ones that failed? What is the control for each
measurement, and has it been shown to fail? And what is in the changelog of withdrawn claims ---
because a laboratory that has withdrawn nothing in a month of AI-assisted work has either done
nothing or is not looking.

\section{Limitations, and what the month did not establish}
\label{sec:limits}

The evidence is two small laboratories, one owner, one month, and one problem area; nothing
here is a controlled comparison against a laboratory run otherwise. The claim is not that the
method produces more correct science than another --- the month produced no theorem about
fluids that the field did not have --- but that it produces errors of a particular, useful
shape, and the evidence for that is the table.

The method is expensive in a way that compounds. Every control needs a control; every
verifier needs a verifier; the lessons file grows faster than any session can read it, and the
externalised context it depends on is itself a document that can be stale. We have no
principled stopping rule for the regress and used judgement, which is the thing the method
exists to constrain.

Statement adequacy remains the unsolved step. Reserving it to a human is the right rule and a
bottleneck; Palomar's language-model check is a scalable filter and not a certificate; and the
month's largest failure sat exactly there. A registry entry, a ledger row and a human audit are
three different things, and a laboratory needs all three.

The autoresearch pattern transferred with reservations. It chooses an implementation, never a
result; its proposers must be denied the code so that checking against the code catches
something; and the one screen we ran misfired on a registered rule. Its winner is a setting,
and needed the ordinary controls before anything downstream cited it.

Finally, this paper was drafted by the same class of system whose failures it catalogues, from
the laboratory's own records, and it was audited by an agent following the procedure of
Section~\ref{sec:agents} against those records. That is the method applied to itself and it is
also a conflict of interest. The reader has the lessons file, the ledger, the changelog and the
repositories, and should prefer them to any sentence here.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Formal verification has given AI-assisted mathematics a trust layer for proofs, and a registry
now makes that layer public. A laboratory needs the same layer for everything that is not a
proof: the choice of question, the instrument, the number, the sentence built on the number,
and the act of publishing it. The method described here supplies that layer with four
instruments that are each mechanical --- a tiered gate, a control discipline, pre-registration,
and a ledger --- and an agent framework organised by the cost of a wrong statement. A month of
running it in two laboratories produced no correct prediction it could not have produced
otherwise, and a record of every error with its date, its cause, its catcher and its rule. Tao's
rule is to rely on the machine only where you can red-team its output. The laboratory's job is
to arrange that the red-teaming is not optional, and to write down what it found.
